A reviewer commented on the draft
This paper investigates whether embodied-learning–oriented robotics videos (hands-on, physical demonstrations) generate
greater learner engagement than conventional, lecture-style robotics videos. The authors analyze a large set of YouTube
robotics tutorials using large language models to (a) caption video frames, (b) analyze user comment sentiment, and (c)
compute correlations between comments and video content as a proxy for engagement.
Across multiple metrics, including comment volume, sentiment, and content–comment alignment, the study finds that
embodied learning videos consistently show higher and more content-aligned user engagement than conventional videos. A
small user study (n=20) further supports these results, indicating stronger interaction and perceived learning effectiveness
for embodied videos. Overall, the paper argues that embodied learning videos are more effective for engaging learners in
robotics instruction and workforce training, and that LLM-based multimodal analysis is a viable method for evaluating
instructional video effectiveness at scale.
While the paper offers a technically sound and methodologically innovative analysis of user engagement with robotics
instructional videos, it stops short of critically interpreting what these engagement differences mean for learning, design, or
theory. The central finding, that embodied videos generate higher engagement, is confirmatory rather than generative, and
the paper does not address the broader question of why this matters or how it should inform future instructional practice or
research.
A reviewer commented on the abstract
This study examines a crucial topic for CoED: the comparison between embedded learning videos, which feature
instructors utilizing body language and other physical cues to illustrate robotic concepts, and traditional lecture-style videos
that rely solely on verbal/slide explanations. The two complementary analyses are interesting. However, the statistical
power of the reported finding is unclear. The authors might want to expand the discussion of statistical methods, including
effect sizes or significance levels. Furthermore, while engagement metrics such as user comments and sentiment analysis
provide valuable insights into students' interaction, this work does not directly demonstrate gains in students' understanding
or robotic concepts or skills. Despite these concerns, I look forward to reviewing the paper's draft and hearing more about
the study.
