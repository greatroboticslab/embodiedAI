\title{Multimodal Fusion Analysis of Visual and Verbal Engagement in Embodied Robotics Instruction}

\author[1,*]{Hongbo Zhang}
\author[1]{Benjamin Li}
\author[1]{Reshma Rajan}
\author[1]{Natalie Galvan}
\affil[1]{Department of Engineering Technology, Middle Tennessee State University, Murfreesboro, TN 37132, USA}

\affil[*]{hbzhang@mtsu.edu}

\begin{abstract}
Prior research on embodied learning in robotics education has relied primarily on transcript-based metrics, which inherently undervalue instructional approaches that communicate through physical demonstration rather than verbal narration. This study introduces a multimodal fusion framework that jointly analyzes visual and verbal channels of engagement at the segment level, and shows that any single-channel metric---transcript or visual---systematically mis-ranks instructional modalities. We analyzed 130 robotics instructional videos (56 conventional, 48 verbally embodied, 26 visually embodied), segmenting each into 10-second intervals and classifying each segment by content type using Gemini 2.0 Flash video understanding. For every segment we computed four engagement metrics: an independent visual correlation, an independent transcript correlation, their union (at least one channel correlated), and their intersection (both channels correlated). The two single-channel metrics produce nearly inverted rankings of content effectiveness: hands-on demonstrations rank first visually (64.1\%) but last verbally (12.5\%), while slide/PowerPoint content ranks first verbally (22.8\%) but sixth visually (47.1\%). Three parallel OLS regressions fit to visual, transcript, and union scores reveal that hand visibility and the visually embodied subgroup have opposite signs on the two channels (hand visibility: $+3.6$ visual, $-2.7$ transcript; visually embodied: $+16.4$ visual, $-10.9$ transcript), while screen/software content is the only content type that is significantly negative on every channel. Two predictors---instructional density and narration---are non-significant in the visual-only regression but emerge as significant in the transcript and union models, demonstrating that a visual-only analysis actively hides effects that dual-channel measurement detects. At the video level, hands-on content proportion correlates positively with visual engagement ($r = +0.333$, $p < 10^{-4}$) but negatively with transcript engagement ($r = -0.353$, $p < 10^{-4}$); only the union metric recovers a net-positive effect ($r = +0.252$). The union metric ranks hands-on demonstrations highest overall (78.5\% of segments correlated), while the intersection metric identifies diagram/whiteboard content as uniquely effective at dual-channel engagement (26.9\%). When the three subgroups are ranked under transcript-only analysis, conventional videos appear first and visually embodied videos last; under visual-only analysis the ranking reverses completely; only union engagement, where visually embodied videos lead at 79.1\% of segments correlated, yields a stable and interpretable comparison. These findings establish that dual-channel measurement is not a refinement of single-channel analysis but a correction: a complete evaluation of instructional video effectiveness requires both channels, because each channel alone reverses the conclusions of the other.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Embodied learning theory posits that cognitive processes are fundamentally grounded in bodily experience, and that physical interaction with learning materials enhances comprehension, retention, and transfer~\cite{wilson2002six,barsalou2008grounded}. In robotics education, this principle manifests as hands-on instruction---hardware assembly, wiring, calibration, and real-time operation---in contrast to conventional lecture-based approaches that rely on slides, diagrams, and verbal explanation~\cite{johnson2014embodied}. While the theoretical advantages of embodied instruction are well established, empirical quantification of how visual and verbal modalities independently contribute to learner engagement remains limited.

Our prior work~\cite{zhang2025transcript} examined engagement in the same corpus of 130 robotics videos using transcript-based semantic correlation. That analysis revealed a confound: embodied videos appeared to underperform conventional videos on transcript metrics because a subset (26 of 74) were silent demonstrations with minimal narration. After isolating verbally narrated embodied videos, the transcript-based correlation gap narrowed substantially, demonstrating that skilled verbal narration of physical procedures can be as effective as conventional lecture delivery for generating topically aligned viewer responses.

However, transcript-only analysis cannot capture the visual dimension of engagement that is central to embodied instruction. When an instructor demonstrates soldering a circuit or calibrating a sensor, the visual content communicates instructional information that the transcript may not encode. Viewer comments responding to what they \emph{see} rather than what they \emph{hear} are invisible to transcript-based metrics. This creates a systematic bias against embodied instruction, whose pedagogical value is disproportionately carried by the visual channel.

To address this limitation, this study introduces a multimodal fusion framework that jointly analyzes visual and verbal engagement at the segment level. Each video is divided into 10-second segments, and viewer comments are correlated with both frame-level visual captions and transcript excerpts independently. This dual-channel approach enables us to measure how much of a viewer's response is driven by visual content versus verbal content, and to identify which specific content types---hands-on demonstrations, equipment close-ups, diagrams, slides, screen recordings---generate the highest engagement.

We further employ large multimodal model-based video classification to categorize each segment's content type, audio-visual alignment, and instructional features directly from the raw video. This segment-level classification enables a shift from group-level comparisons (embodied vs.\ conventional) to a mechanistic analysis of \emph{what} content features drive engagement and \emph{why} embodied videos achieve higher visual correlation scores.

Our central hypothesis is that the engagement advantage of embodied instruction is attributable to the specific visual content it delivers---physical demonstrations, visible hand manipulation, and equipment interaction---rather than to the instructional category label itself. If correct, then (1) content type should remain a significant predictor of engagement after controlling for subgroup, (2) the subgroup effect should diminish or vanish when content type is included as a covariate, and (3) the proportion of hands-on content within a video should predict its overall engagement at the video level.

\subsection*{Content type mediates the subgroup effect}

The previous analyses established two facts: (1) content type predicts engagement, and (2) subgroups differ in content composition. A critical question follows: does the subgroup label carry any independent explanatory power after content type is accounted for? To test this, we conducted a two-way ANOVA with content type and subgroup as factors, including their interaction.

Table~\ref{tab:twoway} presents the results. Content type remains a strong predictor ($F = 39.5$, $p < 10^{-54}$, $\eta^2 = 3.03\%$). However, the subgroup main effect---which was highly significant in the one-way ANOVA ($F = 99.4$, $p < 10^{-43}$)---becomes non-significant when content type is included ($F = 1.7$, $p = 0.191$, $\eta^2 = 0.03\%$). The interaction term is significant ($F = 8.4$, $p < 10^{-19}$, $\eta^2 = 1.29\%$), indicating that the same content type generates different engagement levels depending on the subgroup.

    \label{tab:twoway}

This result provides direct statistical evidence for the mediation hypothesis: the subgroup-level engagement advantage of embodied instruction is entirely attributable to the content types those videos employ. Once content type is controlled, knowing whether a video is ``conventional'' or ``embodied'' provides no additional information about its visual engagement. The significant interaction reflects the finding that embodied instructors produce higher-quality hands-on demonstrations (67.1--67.8\% visual correlation vs.\ 43.9\% for conventional hands-on content), as reported in the subgroup-level analysis below.

The transcript channel reveals a contrasting pattern. A parallel two-way ANOVA on transcript engagement score (Table~\ref{tab:twoway_tr}) shows that the subgroup effect \emph{remains} highly significant ($F = 130.0$, $p < 10^{-29}$, $\eta^2 = 2.50\%$) even after controlling for content type. Content type is also significant ($F = 11.8$, $p < 10^{-14}$, $\eta^2 = 0.91\%$) but with substantially smaller effect size than in the visual model. This asymmetry is revealing: visual engagement is driven by \emph{what} is shown (content type), while transcript engagement is driven by \emph{who} is speaking (subgroup), reflecting fundamental differences in how verbal versus visual information is processed in instructional contexts.

Three within-content-type patterns emerge. First, on the visual channel, both embodied subgroups achieve substantially higher correlation for hands-on content (67.1\% verbally embodied, 67.8\% visually embodied) than conventional videos (43.9\%). A one-way ANOVA on visual engagement score for hands-on segments confirms significant subgroup differences ($F = 44.3$, $p = 1.4 \times 10^{-19}$). Pairwise Welch's $t$-tests show that both embodied subgroups significantly outperform conventional (conventional vs.\ verbally embodied: $t = -8.95$, $p < 10^{-17}$, $d = 0.57$; conventional vs.\ visually embodied: $t = -8.50$, $p < 10^{-16}$, $d = 0.57$), while the two embodied subgroups do not differ ($t = 0.38$, $p = 0.70$). Conventional instructors who attempt hands-on content do not produce segments as engaging as those made by instructors whose primary modality is hands-on demonstration. Second, on the transcript channel, the subgroup ordering is different: verbally embodied hands-on segments achieve the highest transcript correlation (20.1\%), conventional hands-on segments are intermediate (13.7\%), and visually embodied hands-on segments are near zero (1.7\%). A one-way ANOVA on transcript engagement score for hands-on segments confirms significant subgroup differences ($F = 101.3$, $p < 10^{-42}$), with pairwise tests showing that verbally embodied significantly outperforms conventional ($t = -3.62$, $p = 3.1 \times 10^{-4}$, $d = 0.21$) and visually embodied ($t = 15.87$, $p < 10^{-51}$, $d = 0.70$). This ordering reflects the defining characteristic of each subgroup's narration pattern: verbally embodied instructors describe their physical actions actively, conventional instructors narrate their occasional hands-on moments more formally, and visually embodied instructors typically do not narrate at all.

Third, and most importantly for the main finding of this study, the union metric resolves the ambiguity between the two single-channel stories. A one-way ANOVA on union score for hands-on segments is highly significant ($F = 46.4$, $p < 10^{-20}$). Verbally embodied videos achieve the highest union engagement for hands-on content (81.0\%), with visually embodied videos close behind (79.9\%) and conventional videos notably lower (66.3\%). The union metric shows that the visual-only ranking's apparent tie between verbally and visually embodied (67.1\% vs.\ 67.8\%) obscures a real 1.1-point advantage in total engagement for verbally embodied instruction, driven by the transcript channel's 18.4-point gap. This is direct evidence that within-content-type quality differences between subgroups can only be fully characterized with dual-channel measurement: the visual channel ranks two subgroups as equivalent, the transcript channel ranks them very differently, and only the union channel integrates the two views into a coherent ordering.

All three models are highly significant (all $p < 10^{-74}$), with modest $R^2$ values (visual 0.050, transcript 0.038, union 0.042) reflecting the high variance inherent in segment-level engagement data. The side-by-side structure reveals several patterns that a single-channel regression would obscure.

\textbf{Channel-flipping predictors.} Four predictors produce opposite-sign effects across channels. Hands-on demonstration is the strongest positive predictor of visual engagement ($\beta = +6.2$, $p < 0.001$) but a significant \emph{negative} predictor of transcript engagement ($\beta = -2.9$, $p = 0.027$). Hand visibility shows the same flip ($+3.6$ visual, $-2.7$ transcript; both $p < 0.001$), confirming the resource-competition model of attention: when a segment shows physical manipulation, viewers respond to the visual channel at the expense of the verbal. The visually embodied subgroup coefficient flips most dramatically ($+16.4$ visual, $-10.9$ transcript), reflecting that silent-demonstration videos sacrifice transcript engagement in exchange for substantial visual gains. Despite these cross-channel tradeoffs, the union model shows that hands-on ($+3.7$), hand visibility ($+2.7$), and visually embodied subgroup ($+13.2$) all remain net-positive: the visual gains outweigh the transcript losses when total engagement is measured.

\textbf{Uniformly negative predictors.} Screen/software content is the only content type that significantly reduces engagement on \emph{every} channel ($\beta = -13.2$ visual, $-8.6$ transcript, $-16.1$ union; all $p < 10^{-12}$). This identifies it as an unambiguous liability: unlike hands-on or diagram content, which trade engagement between channels, screen recordings fail to engage viewers through either pathway. Device-in-operation content is a weaker version of the same pattern, negative on visual and union but neutral on transcript.

\textbf{Predictors that appear only when transcript is modeled.} Two predictors are statistically non-significant in the visual-only model but emerge as significant when the transcript and union channels are included. Instructional density is non-significant for visual engagement ($p = 0.106$) but significant for both transcript ($\beta = +0.8$, $p = 0.006$) and union ($p = 0.034$): denser instructional content produces measurable transcript-channel engagement gains that a visual-only analysis misses. Narration shows the same pattern: non-significant for visual alone ($p = 0.069$) but significant on the union model ($\beta = +3.8$, $p = 0.023$). Notably, narration does \emph{not} reach significance on the transcript channel alone ($p = 0.213$) after controlling for the other predictors---its contribution to total engagement emerges only through the combined union metric.

\textbf{Subgroup effects remain robust across channels.} The verbally embodied coefficient is significantly positive on all three channels ($+6.0$, $+1.8$, $+6.6$), indicating that even after controlling for content composition, verbally embodied instructors produce higher engagement on every channel. The visually embodied coefficient is the only coefficient with genuinely inverted signs (positive on visual and union, strongly negative on transcript), mirroring the defining feature of this subgroup: physical demonstration without narration.

Taken together, the three-model comparison establishes that the dual-channel framework is not merely a descriptive convenience. It produces conclusions that single-channel regression would miss entirely: (1) the resource-competition tradeoff for hands-on content and hand visibility is only visible with both channels modeled; (2) screen/software content is the only unambiguous negative predictor---a conclusion that strengthens when the union channel is added; and (3) instructional density and narration have measurable engagement effects that the visual channel alone does not detect.

    \label{fig:temporal}

Figure~\ref{fig:temporal} reveals contrasting temporal patterns across subgroups. Conventional videos exhibit a monotonic decline in visual engagement from early (46.6) to late (38.6) segments, a drop of 8.0 points. Verbally embodied videos show a relatively flat trajectory (47.5, 48.2, 45.8), with a slight decline only in the final third. Visually embodied videos display an inverted-U pattern, peaking at 65.4 in the middle portion before returning to 59.3---still substantially higher than either other subgroup at any time point.

The decline in conventional video engagement likely reflects viewer fatigue with sustained lecture-format content. The stability of engagement in embodied videos suggests that physical demonstrations maintain viewer attention over longer durations. The mid-video peak in visually embodied videos may reflect that the core demonstration content typically occupies the middle portion, with introductory and concluding segments being less visually rich.

The transcript channel exhibits a notably different temporal pattern (Table~\ref{tab:temporal_all}). For both conventional and verbally embodied videos, transcript engagement is highest in the early segments (conventional: 22.6, verbally embodied: 21.3) and declines substantially by the late portion (conventional: 15.4, verbally embodied: 14.1). This front-loading of transcript engagement contrasts sharply with the mid-peak pattern observed in visual engagement for embodied videos, and suggests that viewers extract verbal meaning most effectively from the introductory portions where topic framing and definitions typically appear. Visually embodied videos show minimal transcript engagement at all time points (2.1--2.8), consistent with their sparse narration.

Conventional and verbally embodied videos are very close on the union metric (64.6\% vs.\ 66.7\%), consistent with the finding that they have similar transcript engagement and differ modestly on visual engagement. The dual-channel framework introduced here is therefore not an alternative to Paper~1's findings but a correction of their scope: conventional and verbally embodied videos engage viewers through both channels at similar rates, while visually embodied videos engage viewers predominantly through the visual channel. A complete evaluation of instructional video effectiveness requires both channels; reporting only one will systematically favor the modality it measures.

\section*{Discussion}

This study provides the first segment-level, multimodal analysis of viewer engagement in robotics instructional videos, moving beyond group-level comparisons to identify the specific content features that drive learner interaction. Five principal findings emerge.

\textbf{Visual and transcript channels produce inverted rankings of content effectiveness.} The dual-channel analysis reveals that content types which excel visually tend to underperform verbally, and vice versa. Hands-on demonstrations rank first in visual correlation (64.1\%) but last in transcript correlation (12.5\%), while slide/PowerPoint content ranks sixth in visual correlation (47.1\%) but first in transcript correlation (22.8\%). This inversion is not a measurement artifact but a substantive feature of how different content types engage learners: physical demonstrations recruit viewer attention toward the visual channel, while verbal content types anchor viewer comments to the spoken topic. The weak overall correlation between channels ($r = 0.14$) confirms that these are largely independent engagement pathways. Any single-channel analysis will therefore systematically favor one modality over the other---a methodological consequence that motivates the dual-channel framework adopted here.

\textbf{Content type is the primary driver of visual engagement, while subgroup drives transcript engagement.} The two parallel two-way ANOVAs yield strikingly asymmetric results. For the visual channel, content type remains highly significant after controlling for subgroup ($F = 39.5$, $p < 10^{-54}$) while the subgroup main effect becomes non-significant ($F = 1.7$, $p = 0.191$)---full mediation. For the transcript channel, the pattern inverts: subgroup dominates ($F = 130.0$, $p < 10^{-29}$, $\eta^2 = 2.50\%$) while content type has a smaller but still significant effect ($F = 11.8$, $p < 10^{-14}$, $\eta^2 = 0.91\%$). Together these results suggest that \emph{what} is shown drives visual engagement, while \emph{who} is speaking (and how much) drives transcript engagement. The similarity in transcript engagement between conventional and verbally embodied videos (20.4\% vs.\ 19.9\%) further indicates that when embodied instructors do narrate, their verbal content engages viewers at nearly the same rate as conventional lecturers---a finding consistent with the verbal-embodied-teaching thesis developed in Paper~1~\cite{zhang2025transcript}.

\textbf{Union and intersection metrics reveal that hands-on content and diagrams are uniquely effective for different reasons.} The union metric (at least one channel correlated) identifies hands-on demonstrations as the most engaging content type overall (78.5\%), confirming that the visual dominance of this content type more than compensates for its weak transcript engagement. The intersection metric (both channels correlated) tells a different story: diagram/whiteboard content achieves the highest dual-channel correlation (26.9\%), exceeding hands-on demonstrations (15.5\%) by 11 percentage points. Diagrams are uniquely effective at simultaneously engaging both channels because instructors typically describe diagrams verbally while pointing to visual features---a natural dual-channel coupling. Hands-on content, by contrast, operates predominantly through the visual channel alone. These complementary metrics suggest distinct instructional affordances: hands-on content maximizes total engagement bandwidth, while diagrams maximize cross-channel reinforcement.

\textbf{The engagement advantage scales from segments to videos.} The video-level analysis confirms that the segment-level content type effect translates to overall video engagement. Among verbally embodied videos, the correlation between percentage of hands-on content and mean visual engagement is $r = 0.53$ ($p < 10^{-4}$). The top-quartile engaged verbally embodied videos contain 3.1 times more hands-on content than the bottom quartile (40.4\% vs.\ 12.9\%). These convergent results across multiple levels of analysis strengthen the causal interpretation: it is the hands-on content, not a confound, that drives engagement. Visually embodied videos achieve the highest union engagement (79.1\%) of any subgroup despite near-zero transcript engagement (2.8\%), demonstrating that a single strong channel can sustain total viewer engagement when the other is absent.

\textbf{Channel tradeoffs govern the effect of narration and hand visibility.} Several segment features produce opposing effects on the two channels. Hand visibility boosts visual correlation by 12.0 percentage points ($p < 10^{-40}$) but reduces transcript correlation by 4.8 points ($p < 10^{-13}$)---segments with visible hands draw attention toward physical manipulation and away from verbal content. Narration shows a similar tradeoff: narrated segments have slightly lower visual correlation but substantially higher transcript correlation, yet the union engagement remains nearly identical (67.0\% vs.\ 67.9\%). This conservation of total engagement across narration conditions is a novel finding: narration does not add to or subtract from total viewer engagement, but redistributes it between channels. This supports a resource-competition model of attention in instructional video viewing, where viewers allocate limited attentional resources across modalities rather than processing them independently.

The temporal analysis adds further nuance: conventional video visual engagement declines monotonically from early to late segments ($46.6 \to 38.6$), suggesting viewer fatigue with sustained lecture content. Embodied video visual engagement remains stable or peaks mid-video, indicating that physical demonstrations sustain attention over longer durations. The transcript channel, in contrast, is strongly front-loaded across both narrated subgroups, with transcript engagement in the early third (22.6 conventional, 21.3 verbally embodied) roughly 50\% higher than in the late third (15.4 conventional, 14.1 verbally embodied). Viewers appear to extract verbal meaning most effectively from the introductory framing of a video, while visual engagement patterns depend on the pacing of physical demonstrations.

\textbf{Practical implications.} Instructors seeking to maximize visual engagement should prioritize hands-on demonstration content, ensure that physical manipulations are visually prominent (hands in frame), and minimize extended screen recording segments. The finding that screen/software content consistently ranks lowest across all analyses---lowest visual correlation, strongest negative regression coefficient, most prevalent in bottom-quartile videos---identifies a specific content type that should be used sparingly or interleaved with other formats. For conventional instruction where hands-on content is impractical, diagrams and whiteboard explanations provide the strongest visual engagement.

\textbf{Limitations.} The content classification was performed by a single large multimodal model (Gemini 2.0 Flash), and inter-rater reliability with human coders was not assessed. The correlation metrics depend on viewer comments that were elicited at the video level, then matched to individual segments, potentially introducing aggregation artifacts. The modest regression $R^2$ (0.050) indicates that content type and segment features explain only a fraction of the total variance in engagement; individual viewer differences, topic interest, and video production quality likely account for much of the unexplained variance. The audio-visual alignment score showed weak correlation with engagement ($r = 0.04$), which may reflect limitations in the alignment metric itself. Future work should incorporate human validation of content classifications, control for topic and production quality, and develop finer-grained measures of audio-visual synchrony.

\subsection*{Dataset}

The dataset consists of 130 robotics instructional YouTube videos: 56 conventional and 74 embodied (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_examples}). Embodied videos present hands-on tutorials involving hardware assembly, wiring, calibration, and real-time robot operation. Based on analysis of speech rate distribution~\cite{zhang2025transcript}, the 74 embodied videos were subdivided into 48 verbally embodied ($\geq 60$ words per minute) and 26 visually embodied ($< 60$ WPM, silent demonstrations with minimal narration). This three-way classification---conventional ($n = 56$), verbally embodied ($n = 48$), and visually embodied ($n = 26$)---is maintained throughout all analyses.

\textbf{Stage 1: Frame extraction.}
Frames are extracted from each video using OpenCV at a rate of one frame per 100 video frames (approximately one frame every 3.3 seconds at 30~fps). Videos encoded in AV1 codec are automatically transcoded to H.264 prior to extraction. The extracted frames serve as input to the visual captioning pipeline.

\textbf{Stage 2: Audio transcription.} Audio is transcribed using OpenAI Whisper~\cite{radford2023robust} with the \texttt{turbo} model, producing timestamped segments with start and end times in seconds. The timestamped output enables precise alignment between transcript segments and 10-second video intervals.

\textbf{Stage 3: Visual captioning.} Each extracted frame is independently captioned by two vision-language models: LLaVA~\cite{liu2023visual} and MiniCPM~\cite{yao2024minicpm}, both served locally via Ollama. The captioning prompt instructs the model to describe visual content objectively, focusing on visible elements (devices, slides, diagrams, text, hand positions) without inferring topics or intentions. The two captions per frame are then integrated into a single comprehensive caption using LLaMA~3~\cite{touvron2023llama}, which resolves contradictions and removes redundancy while preserving all factual observations from both sources. This dual-captioning approach improves coverage: LLaVA tends to capture spatial relationships and scene composition, while MiniCPM provides finer detail on text and small objects.

\textbf{Stage 4: Temporal alignment and segmentation.}
Integrated captions and transcript segments are aligned on a shared timeline. Each caption is assigned a timestamp based on its frame index and the video's framerate. The aligned stream is then divided into fixed 10-second intervals, with each interval receiving all captions and transcript segments whose timestamps fall within its time window. This produces a uniform segmentation across all videos, with each segment containing both a visual summary (concatenated frame captions) and a verbal summary (concatenated transcript text).

\textbf{Stage 5: Dual-channel correlation scoring.}
For each segment, viewer comments are independently compared against the visual captions and the transcript excerpt using LLaMA~3 via Ollama. Two separate prompts are used:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual correlation prompt:} Instructs the model to determine whether a viewer comment refers to the visual content shown in the segment's frame captions. The model returns a boolean correlation judgment and a confidence score (0--100).
    \item \textbf{Transcript correlation prompt:} Instructs the model to determine whether a viewer comment discusses the speech or topic conveyed in the segment's transcript. The model returns a boolean correlation judgment and a confidence score (0--100).
\end{itemize}

Viewer comments were collected from two sources: (1) YouTube public comments retrieved via the YouTube Data API, and (2) student reflective responses submitted after viewing the videos during scheduled lecture sessions~\cite{zhang2025transcript}. To manage computational cost, a pre-filtering step using sentence-transformer embeddings (\texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2}) selects the top 200 most semantically relevant comments per video before correlation scoring. Each selected comment is scored against every segment, producing a matrix of visual and transcript correlation scores per video.

\textbf{Stage 6: Metric aggregation.}
Per-segment metrics are computed by aggregating across all comment--segment pairs: visual correlation percentage (fraction of pairs where the LLM classified the comment as related to the visual content), mean visual score, transcript correlation percentage (fraction classified as related to the spoken content), and mean transcript score. These segment-level metrics form the basis for all statistical analyses.


\subsection*{Video content classification}

Independent of the fusion pipeline, each video was classified at the segment level using Gemini 2.0 Flash~\cite{geminiteam2024gemini2} with direct video understanding. Unlike the fusion pipeline, which processes extracted frames and transcripts, the classification pipeline uploads the raw video file to the Gemini File API. The model watches the actual video content and classifies each 10-second segment.

To maintain output reliability, videos are processed in 5-minute chunks (approximately 30 segments per chunk). The classification prompt specifies the exact number of segments expected per chunk to enforce one-to-one correspondence with the fusion analysis segments. For each segment, the model assigns:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Content type}: One of nine mutually exclusive categories---\emph{hands\_on\_demonstration}, \emph{equipment\_closeup}, \emph{presenter\_talking}, \emph{slide\_or\_powerpoint}, \emph{diagram\_or\_whiteboard}, \emph{screen\_or\_software}, \emph{animation\_or\_graphic}, \emph{device\_in\_operation}, or \emph{other}. These categories were designed to span the full range of content observed in robotics instructional videos while maintaining mutual exclusivity.
    \item \textbf{Alignment score} (0--100): Degree to which the audio narration matches the visual content being shown. A score of 100 indicates perfect alignment (e.g., describing exactly what is on screen); a score near 0 indicates a mismatch (e.g., narrating theory while showing equipment).
    \item \textbf{Narration present}: Boolean indicating whether the instructor is actively speaking during the segment.
    \item \textbf{Hands visible}: Boolean indicating whether the instructor's hands appear in the frame, operationalizing the degree of physical interaction.
    \item \textbf{Instructional density} (1--5): Rating of how much instructional content is conveyed in the segment, from minimal (1, e.g., pauses, transitions) to maximal (5, e.g., dense technical explanation with demonstration).
\end{enumerate}

The classification was run on the Pittsburgh Supercomputing Center's Bridges-2 system via SLURM batch processing. Resume logic saves progress after each chunk, enabling recovery from API timeouts. Retry logic (3 attempts with exponential backoff at 30/60/90 seconds) handles transient server errors. A total of 9{,}997 segments across 129 videos were successfully classified. One 96-minute video exceeded Gemini's 1-million-token context limit and was excluded.


\subsection*{Data integration}

The fusion pipeline (Stages 1--6) and the classification pipeline produce independent per-segment data that are joined on video identifier and segment index. The joined dataset contains 9{,}997 observations, each with visual and transcript engagement metrics from the fusion pipeline and content type, narration, hand visibility, alignment, and density ratings from the classification pipeline. Video-level metrics are computed by averaging segment-level scores within each video ($N = 128$ videos with complete data from both pipelines).


\subsection*{Engagement metrics}

For each video, the correlation scoring stage (Stage~5) produces a matrix of judgments: every selected comment is evaluated against every 10-second segment on both channels independently. We define the following per-segment engagement metrics used throughout the Results.

\textbf{Per-pair correlation rate.}
For a given segment $s$ with $C$ scored comments, the visual correlation rate and
transcript correlation rate are

\[
V_s = C^{-1}\sum_{c=1}^{C} \mathbf{1}[\text{comment } c \text{ is visually correlated with } s],
\]

\[
T_s = C^{-1}\sum_{c=1}^{C} \mathbf{1}[\text{comment } c \text{ is transcript-correlated with } s],
\]

where $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function derived from the LLM's binary judgment.
These per-pair rates range from 0 to 100\% and represent the fraction of comments that
engage with each channel of that segment. When averaged across segments (e.g., by content
type or subgroup), they yield the “Vis.\ Corr.\%” and “Tr.\ Corr.\%” columns reported in all tables.

\textbf{Engagement scores.} Alongside each binary judgment, the LLM provides a confidence score $\in [0, 100]$. The visual engagement score for segment $s$ is $\bar{v}_s = C^{-1}\sum_{c=1}^{C} v_{c,s}$ and the transcript engagement score is $\bar{t}_s = C^{-1}\sum_{c=1}^{C} t_{c,s}$, where $v_{c,s}$ and $t_{c,s}$ are the per-pair confidence values. These continuous scores are used as the dependent variable in ANOVA and regression analyses, as they provide finer discrimination than binary rates.

\textbf{Union and intersection metrics.} Two segment-level binary metrics capture cross-channel engagement. A segment is \emph{union-correlated} if at least one comment is correlated on either channel: $U_s = \mathbf{1}[V_s > 0 \lor T_s > 0]$. A segment is \emph{intersection-correlated} (``Both'') if at least one comment is correlated on each channel independently: $B_s = \mathbf{1}[V_s > 0 \land T_s > 0]$. The union and intersection percentages reported in tables (``Union Seg.\%'' and ``Both Seg.\%'') are the fraction of segments satisfying each condition within a group.

\textbf{Union engagement score.} For the regression and video-level analyses, we define the union score as $u_s = \max(\bar{v}_s, \bar{t}_s)$, the per-segment maximum of the two channel scores. This continuous metric captures the best-channel engagement for each segment and serves as the dependent variable in the union regression model.

\textbf{Distinction between per-pair rates and segment-level binary metrics.} Per-pair rates ($V_s$, $T_s$) measure the \emph{proportion} of comments engaging with a segment on a given channel; segment-level binary metrics ($U_s$, $B_s$) measure whether \emph{any} engagement occurs. These are complementary: a segment can have low per-pair visual correlation (e.g., 5\% of comments) yet still count as union-correlated. All tables specify which metric type is reported.



\subsection*{Statistical analysis}

All statistical tests were conducted at the segment level ($N = 9{,}997$) unless otherwise noted. One-way ANOVA was used to test for differences in visual, transcript, and union engagement across content types (9 levels), subgroups (3 levels), and temporal bins (3 levels). Within-content-type ANOVAs tested whether the same content type (e.g., hands-on demonstration) produces different engagement across subgroups. A two-way ANOVA with content type and subgroup as factors (including their interaction) was used to test whether the subgroup effect persists after controlling for content type; partial eta-squared ($\eta^2$) was computed as the effect size for each factor.

A Pearson chi-square test of independence was used to test whether the distribution of content types across subgroups differs from the null expectation of equal proportions, with Cram\'{e}r's $V$ as the effect size.

Independent-samples Welch's $t$-tests were used for pairwise comparisons between specific content types and between segment feature conditions (e.g., narration present vs.\ absent, hands visible vs.\ not), with Cohen's $d$ reported as the effect size. Pearson correlation was computed between the alignment score and engagement metrics, between hands-on content proportion and video-level engagement, and between visual and transcript engagement scores (channel independence).

Three parallel ordinary least squares (OLS) multiple regression models were fit to predict visual, transcript, and union engagement scores from the same set of predictors: content type (dummy-coded, reference: slide/PowerPoint), hand visibility, narration, instructional density, and subgroup (dummy-coded, reference: conventional). Fitting the same predictors against all three dependent variables reveals how each factor contributes to each channel independently.

For the top/bottom quartile analysis, videos within each subgroup were ranked by mean union engagement score, and the content type distributions of the top and bottom 25\% were compared using independent-samples $t$-tests. Temporal analysis divided each video into three equal-length bins based on normalized segment position (0--0.33, 0.33--0.67, 0.67--1.0).

All $p$-values are reported without correction for multiple comparisons, as each test addresses a pre-specified hypothesis. Analyses were conducted using Python (scipy, statsmodels, pandas) on the Pittsburgh Supercomputing Center's Bridges-2 system.
